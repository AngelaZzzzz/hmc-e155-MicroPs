Logic Gate Demo Board

Design Process
Prototyping
Digital Logic
A large, durable, lecture-hall-visible logic gate demonstration board designed for Prof. Harris’s E85 class: switches, gates, LEDs, and an adjustable clock on a laser-cut plywood panel.
Author

Angela Zheng

Published

May 1, 2025

Course · E4 Introduction to Engineering Design, Harvey Mudd College

Timeline · Spring 2025

Team · Team Logic: Angela Zheng, Brave Yonghiphatwong, Luke Murphy, Magz Brooke-Hodge, Pierce Clark

Client · Prof. David Harris, for E85 Digital Electronics & Computer Engineering

Tools · SolidWorks, laser cutter, logic ICs, breadboarding

The problem

Prof. Harris teaches logic gates to roughly 70 students in E85 with a demo board, and the old one was falling apart, with cables coming loose. He needed a replacement that every student could read from the back row of Shanahan B460, that held up to being carried to class, and that stayed simple to wire live during lecture.

We turned client interviews into four objectives (durability, visibility, portability, and ease of use) and a set of hard constraints:

Prototyping

We built a separate prototype to answer each of our biggest open questions.

1 · How should we attach wires so they survive being carried around?

We attached wires to plywood coupons five ways: super glue, epoxy, duct tape, staples, and hot glue. Then we put each through abrasion against wood and iron, five drops each from 2, 3, and 4 ft, and repeated tugging. The super-glued and epoxied wires came off. Tape and staples survived but could conduct or snag, so hot glue won.

2 · Can students actually see it from the back row?

We built a full-size foam mock-up with real switches, LEDs, and laser-cut labels and set it at the front of B460. Each team member scored visibility from 0 to 5 at every row. The switches and LEDs were fine, but the gate labels and the 0/1, Vcc and GND markings were unreadable past the first couple of rows, so the labels needed to be much bigger and the gates easier to tell apart.

3 · Does the circuit work?

We drew the full circuit, including the clock, and breadboarded the switches, gates and LEDs before committing to the final layout.

Final design

  • Board: laser-cut, laser-engraved plywood, modeled in SolidWorks so every hole and label lined up with its component.
  • Visibility: each gate type is a large, painted, color-coded shape (blue AND, green OR, red NOT, yellow XOR). Light-up rings make each switch’s state obvious, and the LEDs are bright.
  • Connections: conductive bolts at every gate pin, so Prof. Harris can wire circuits live with the alligator clips he’s used to. Components are bolted and hot-glued on the back.
  • Power and clock: a battery pack replaced our original USB-C plan, which our advisor flagged as not durable enough, plus a rotary knob that sets the clock rate.

Evaluation

We repeated the B460 visibility test with the finished, powered board and eight observers, standing directly in front of the board and at random seats on each side.

The final board scored above 3 in every row, up from 0 in the back row for the mock-up. Every component stayed visible and the gates could be told apart by shape and color throughout the room. The engraved text labels still faded by rows 4–6, especially when the LEDs and switch rings were lit.

What I’d change

  • Weight: the board came in 1.47 lb over its 5 lb target, mostly from the bolts. Lighter hardware and thinner label stock would close the gap.
  • Stiffness: the plywood flexes slightly, so a thicker panel or a composite would hold up better over years of use.
  • Labels: the engraved text needs to be larger and higher contrast to read from the back row.

The board kept everything Prof. Harris liked about the old one, alligator-clip wiring included, while making it far more visible and durable for future E85 students.
